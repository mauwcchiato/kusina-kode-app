Chapter 1 · Introduction background · objectives · scope

Background
  • Filipino cuisine carries regional identity, but learning content is scattered across videos, blogs, and social media.
  • Pre-development survey (n = 64): YouTube 68.8%, family 64.1%, TikTok 59.4%; mobile apps least used, 7.8%.
  • Chef Karen Nina B. Gicana: popular apps (Tasty, Pinterest) are international; Mindanao cuisine (Maranao, Tausug) is underrepresented.
  • Gamified apps keep points and badges as editable records with no proof they are genuine (Motepalli & Jacobsen, 2021).
Research question
  • How can a blockchain-based, gamified Filipino culinary word-puzzle app support culinary vocabulary and heritage learning, keep users engaged, and secure their rewards through a tamper-evident blockchain mechanism?
Objectives
  • A · a module for culinary vocabulary, recipe-based learning, and cultural awareness through word puzzles
  • B · a platform that encourages motivation, engagement, and continued participation through gamification
  • C · blockchain technology to manage rewards, achievements, and activity records securely and verifiably (main contribution)
Scope
  • Android app (Android 8.0+, Kotlin and Jetpack Compose) for players; web admin console (ASP.NET Web Forms) for administrators.
  • Features: word puzzle with color-coded hints · heritage and recipe card after each round · points, badges, leaderboard, progress tracking · blockchain incentive system (private Proof-of-Authority ledger, KK Coins, TxHash receipts) · role-based user management.
Delimitations
  • Filipino cuisine only; recipes are reference, not hands-on cooking instruction.
  • Does not measure how effective the platform is at promoting learning.
  • Does not cover regulation, taxation, or legal compliance of digital assets.
  • Ledger is tamper-evident, not decentralized: one validator key held by the research team; KK Coins are not traded publicly.
Conceptual framework
  • Input-Process-Output. Input: review of literature, Filipino culinary knowledge base, chef interview, survey.
  • Process: Agile Scrum development, with blockchain incentive integration, admin console deployment, and usability testing.
  • Output: the Kusina Kode mobile app, the admin console, and the blockchain-based reward and verification system.

Panel might ask: "Did you measure whether students learned more?" No; it is a delimitation. The study measures acceptance, quality, and usability; learning gains are the final recommendation.

Chapter 2 · Review of Related Literature five themes · synthesis

1 Culinary heritage
  • Recipes carry cultural heritage; regional dishes are fading (Gelera et al., 2023); policy concern: Senate Bill No. 244, the Philippine Culinary Heritage Act.
  • Technology can preserve and teach heritage (Partarakis et al., 2021); local studies describe no structured digital tool for Filipino culinary heritage.
2 Gamified learning
  • Gamification raises motivation, engagement, and performance (Oliveira et al., 2023; Jaramillo-Mediavilla et al., 2024; Lampropoulos & Sidiropoulos, 2024).
  • Balance intrinsic and extrinsic motivation; rewards must feel meaningful (John et al., 2023; Baah et al., 2023).
  • Existing culinary apps target Western or general audiences and skip heritage.
3 Blockchain incentives
  • Hashing, signatures, and hash-linked blocks make records tamper-evident; Proof-of-Authority uses approved validators.
  • Public platforms compared (Polygon, Ethereum, Solana, Hyperledger Fabric); a private PoA ledger avoids wallets and fees for players.
  • Blockchain rewards in education increase trust and participation (Khanna, 2025).
4 Architecture and security
  • Cloud models (IaaS, PaaS) on Microsoft Azure; MVVM and Clean Architecture for the app.
  • AES-256, SHA-256, ECDSA; zero-trust API protection (Chandramouli & Butcher, 2025); NIST frameworks.
5 Evaluation and testing
  • ISO/IEC 25010 software quality model; System Usability Scale (Brooke, 1996; 68 = average, Bangor et al., 2008); user acceptance testing.
  • 5.4 Software Testing Approaches: testing levels (ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-1); response-time limits 0.1 s / 1 s / 10 s (Miller, 1968; Nielsen, 1993); load testing (Jiang & Hassan, 2015); 16 ms frames (Google, n.d.); NIST SP 800-115 and OWASP Top 10; Android fragmentation (Wei et al., 2016).
Synthesis
  • No existing platform combines blockchain incentives, gamified learning, and Filipino culinary heritage (Table 1). That gap is the study's contribution.

Panel might ask: "Why not use Polygon or Ethereum?" Players would need wallets, seed phrases, and fees, and KK Coins are not meant to be traded; a private PoA ledger gives tamper-evidence without those costs.

Chapter 3 · Methodology design · Scrum · architecture · testing · analysis

Research design
  • Quantitative, descriptive-evaluative, developmental: build the system, then evaluate it against quality and usability benchmarks.
Scrum
  • Roles: Product Owner Chef Gicana · Scrum Master Jhan Maurice De Roxas · Development Team (Yowanna also QA tester).
  • Events: sprint planning, sprint review, sprint retrospective; 12 sprints.
Cloud and security

  • CIA triad in three layers: cryptographic data protection (TLS, AES-256-GCM) · blockchain tamper-evidence (SHA-256, ECDSA) · API access control (bearer tokens, zero trust).
  • Three-tier architecture: Client tier · Middleware and Cloud Services tier · Blockchain Ledger tier.
System testing
  • Unit · integration · UI (Espresso) · security (Semgrep, OWASP ZAP, brute force, SQL injection) · stress · cloud deployment · browser and mobile compatibility.
Respondents
  • Locale: Mapúa Malayan Colleges Laguna. Expert validator (1, purposive) · Cohort A: 20 Culinary Arts (13) and Hospitality Management (7) students, purposive · Cohort B: 40 students from other programs, convenience.
  • Data gathering in three stages: pre-development survey · expert consultation (semi-structured) · system evaluation (23 test cases for the expert; ISO/IEC 25010 and SUS for all).

Unit 1

Object-oriented programming & architecture

The app is written in Kotlin and the admin console in C#; both are object-oriented. In the app alone there are 114 data classes, 15 interfaces, 16 enums, 59 singleton objects and 8 ViewModels.

1.1 Classes and objects data class · enum · object

Definition

A class is a blueprint that bundles data (properties) with the behaviour that works on it (methods). An object is one instance made from that blueprint.

Why it matters here

Kusina Kode handles many "things" with their own data: levels, players, badges, ingredients, attempts. Modelling each as a class keeps its data and rules together and consistent everywhere they are used.

How we applied it
  • Data classes for records, e.g. PlayerProgress (points, streaks, solved levels) and LevelData. Kotlin generates equality and copying for them, so current.copy(totalPoints = …) makes a new updated record safely.
  • Enums for fixed choices: Region (Luzon, Visayas, Mindanao), Rarity, TileState (Correct, SemiCorrect, Wrong), PowerUp.
  • Singleton objects for one shared rulebook: WordleEngine, ScoreRules, ChefRank, IngredientCatalog.
In plain words
A class is the recipe card; an object is the dish you cook from it. Every level in the game is cooked from the same "level" recipe, so they all have a word, a region and ingredients.
Insights

Data classes are immutable by habit in the app: progress is replaced with copy() rather than edited in place, which avoids half-updated scores when several screens read the same data.

Enums stop impossible values: a tile can only be one of three states, so a typo like "corect" can't compile.

Panel might ask: "Why use a singleton for the scoring rules?" Because there must be exactly one rulebook; two copies could score the same round differently.

1.2 Encapsulation private · read-only views

Definition

Hiding an object's internal state and exposing only controlled ways to read or change it.

Why it matters here

Game state (the board, the timer, the KK balance) must only change through the game's rules; a screen should never be able to set "you won" directly.

How we applied it
// GameViewModel.kt
private val _uiState = MutableStateFlow(…)   // only the ViewModel can change it
val uiState = _uiState.asStateFlow()         // screens get a read-only view
  • The same pattern guards PantryViewModel, KkBalance and the gamification store (private set on gamification in KusinaKodeApp).
  • On the web, ASP.NET pages reach data only through the composition root and service classes (DishService, IngredientService), never straight to SQL.
In plain words
The kitchen has one door for orders. Diners can see the food, but only the cook can change it.
Insights

This is also a security property: if the screen can't change the score, a bug in a screen can't award free KK. The server re-checks everything anyway (see 4.2).

1.3 Abstraction interfaces

Definition

Describing what something does through an interface while hiding how it does it.

Why it matters here

The game needs data from several places: the phone's storage, the PHP API, the chain. The game logic shouldn't care which one answered.

How we applied it
  • 15 repository interfaces, e.g. GamificationRepository, PantryRepository, LevelRepository, AuthRepository, WalletRepository.
  • The coordinator only knows the interface: GamificationCoordinator(repository: GamificationRepository, …).
  • The web console does the same with IIngredientStore, IReelStore and IAdminSignIn in Web_KusinaKode/Abstractions.
In plain words
The game asks "give me the pantry". It doesn't need to know whether the answer came from the phone or from the server.
Insights

Abstraction is what made the unit tests possible: tests swap in fake repositories, so ProgressRules and BadgeRules are tested without a phone or internet.

It also let the team move from local XAMPP to Azure without rewriting the game logic.

1.4 Inheritance extends Android & ASP.NET base classes

Definition

A class reusing and extending another class's behaviour (a subclass "is a" kind of its parent).

Why it matters here

Android and ASP.NET already provide lifecycle, memory and request handling. Inheriting from their base classes gives Kusina Kode those features for free.

How we applied it
  • class KusinaKodeApp : Application(): runs once when the app starts (sets up the network status, gamification store, chain coordinator).
  • class GameViewModel(…) : ViewModel() and seven other ViewModels: survive screen rotation and are cleared automatically.
  • Each admin page (Levels.aspx.cs, Audit.aspx.cs…) inherits from ASP.NET's Page; the shared Admin.master layout gives every page the sidebar and sign-in check.
In plain words
Instead of building our own kitchen, we rented one and added our own recipes.
Insights

The code favours composition over deep inheritance: our own classes rarely extend each other. They combine small parts (a repository, a coordinator) instead, which keeps them easier to test and change.

1.5 Polymorphism one interface, many forms

Definition

One interface or type can stand for many concrete forms, and the right behaviour is chosen at run time.

Why it matters here

Some features have several sources or outcomes: the same "progress" can be stored locally or remotely, and a guess can be valid or invalid in several ways.

How we applied it
  • Interface polymorphism: LocalGamificationRepository, RemotePantryRepository, RemoteAttemptRepository are concrete forms of their repository interfaces.
  • Sealed types: WordleEngine.GuessValidation is Valid, Incomplete, WrongLength(expected, actual) or NotLetters. The compiler forces every screen to handle all four.
  • NotificationGlyph is either Art (a bundled picture) or Remote (a web picture), drawn differently.
In plain words
"Serve the dish" works for adobo or sinigang; each one knows how it should be plated.
Insights

Sealed types turn "did we handle every case?" into a compile error instead of a crash. When a new validation case is added, every place that forgot it stops compiling.

1.6 MVVM, Repository & Clean Architecture design patterns

Definition

MVVM separates the View (screen), ViewModel (state and actions) and Model (data and rules). The Repository pattern hides data sources behind one class. Clean Architecture arranges code in layers where inner rules never depend on outer details. The Observer pattern lets screens react automatically when data changes.

Why it matters here

The project rule (CLAUDE.md) is that screens never call the network or database directly. Four people work on different modules, so clear layers prevent stepping on each other.

How we applied it
  • App: Compose screens → ViewModels exposing StateFlow (observer) → repositories → KusinaApi (Ktor HTTP client). Pure rules live in domain/ with no Android code.
  • Web console: Kyla split it into KusinaKode.Domain (rules, with tests), Application (services), Data (stores) and Presentation (pages), wired in one composition root.
In plain words
The waiter (screen) takes the order, the head cook (ViewModel) decides what happens, and the pantry staff (repository) fetch ingredients. Nobody does someone else's job.
Insights

Because the rules are separate, the same tile-scoring and XP logic is unit-tested on its own (WordleEngineTest, ScoreRulesTest, ProgressRulesTest). Defect D-01 (wrong colours for repeated letters) was found and fixed in that layer.

Panel might ask: "Why not let screens call the API?" A slow network would freeze the screen, and every screen would duplicate error handling. The ViewModel layer does both in one place.

Unit 2

Data structures

Choosing the right container decides how fast the app can find, check and store things. Each structure below is used on purpose.

2.1 Lists and arrays the guess grid

Definition

An ordered collection where each element has a position (index 0, 1, 2…).

Why it matters here

A guess is a row of letters, and each letter's colour depends on its position. Order is the whole point.

How we applied it

WordleEngine.evaluate() returns List<TileState>, one state per letter, built with MutableList(ans.length) { TileState.Wrong }. The board is a list of these rows: six rows for every dish (MAX_ATTEMPTS = 6).

In plain words
A row of numbered boxes. Box 1 checks letter 1, box 2 checks letter 2, and so on.
Insights

The list is sized from the answer's length, not fixed. That is how the project meets its variable word length requirement: ADOBO makes 5 boxes, SINIGANG makes 8, with no special cases.

2.2 Hash maps key → value in one step

Definition

A map stores key-value pairs and finds a value from its key in roughly constant time, O(1), no matter how many entries it holds.

Why it matters here

The app constantly looks things up by name or id: a letter's remaining count, an ingredient's picture, an ingredient's details.

How we applied it
  • remaining = mutableMapOf<Char, Int>() in the tile algorithm counts unused answer letters.
  • IngredientArt.byName maps about 200 dataset ingredient names to their photos.
  • IngredientCatalog.byId maps ids like ing_tuna_fresh to ingredient records.
In plain words
A labelled spice rack: you go straight to "garlic" instead of opening every jar.
Insights

Scanning a list of 123 ingredients for every tile on a dish page would repeat that work for each tile; a map answers each lookup at once. Maps also made the 1.23 fix simple: when a web name isn't in the picture map, the app looks it up by id instead.

2.3 Sets membership without duplicates

Definition

A collection of unique items, built for fast "is X in here?" checks.

Why it matters here

"Has this player already solved this dish?" decides whether a win pays XP and KK. It must be fast and must never count a dish twice.

How we applied it

PlayerProgress.solvedLevels is a Set<Int>. In ProgressRules.advance(): if (event.levelId in current.solvedLevels) return current, so a replay changes nothing. regionsSolved and newIngredientIds are sets too.

In plain words
A stamp card: each dish gets one stamp, and stamping it again doesn't add a second.
Insights

This set is the anti-farming rule. Defect D-05 ("replaying a solved dish awarded points") was fixed by checking this set first. The stress test confirmed it: replays showed "Already solved, +0".

2.4 Streams and queues StateFlow · event bus · chain queue

Definition

A queue processes items in arrival order (first in, first out). A stream (flow) delivers a sequence of values over time to whoever is listening.

Why it matters here

Many things happen after a win: XP, badges, KK, the chain receipt, the palayok. They must happen in order, even if the player is already on another screen.

How we applied it
  • Screens observe StateFlow streams; when the value changes, the screen redraws.
  • GameEvents / GamificationEvents broadcast "round completed", and GamificationCoordinator listens for the whole app lifetime.
  • ChainQueue lines up reward transactions for the ledger; on the server, a job settles anything still pending every 15 minutes.
In plain words
Orders go on a rail in the kitchen and are cooked one after another, so none are lost when it gets busy.
Insights

Defect D-31 ("two rewards at once both fail") is exactly the problem queues solve: two writes racing for the same ledger. Ordering them, plus the server-side settle job, removes the race.

2.5 Hash-linked chain blocks.jsonl

Definition

A linked list where each element (block) stores the hash of the previous block, so the whole list is chained together. This is the core data structure of a blockchain.

Why it matters here

The study's third objective is a tamper-resistant reward record. Every KK earned or spent must be provable later.

How we applied it

The server keeps the chain in /var/lib/kusinakode/chain/blocks.jsonl, one block per line, with each transaction in txs/. lib/chain.php computes each block's hash and checks the chain's tip; the database stores only receipts (tx_ref in reward_claims, shop_unlocks, ingredient_draws).

In plain words
A receipt book where each page carries a fingerprint of the page before it. Tear out or change one page and every page after it stops matching.
Insights

Why a file and not a SQL table? Because the database is meant to be the editable working copy, while the chain is the evidence. An admin can edit a table, but cannot silently edit a block without breaking every later hash. The hourly integrity check on the Audit page detects exactly that.

2.6 Relational tables 20 tables · keys · relationships

Definition

Data stored in tables of rows and columns, linked by primary keys (unique id per row) and foreign keys (a column pointing at another table's key).

Why it matters here

Players, levels, attempts, rewards and ingredients all relate to each other, and the admin console, analytics and leaderboard query across them.

How we applied it

Azure SQL holds 20 tables (see the ERD). Examples: attempts.user_id → users.id, attempts.level_id → levels.id, enforced with ON DELETE CASCADE. Composite keys stop duplicates, e.g. reward_claims' key is (user_id, kind, claim_key), so one first-dish bonus per player per dish.

In plain words
Linked spreadsheets: the attempts sheet doesn't copy player names, it just points to the player's row.
Insights

The composite key on reward_claims makes double payouts impossible at the database level, not just in code. Cascading deletes explain a real event: deleting a level also deleted its attempts, which is why the console now refuses to remove played levels.

Unit 3

Algorithms

The step-by-step procedures behind the game. Each one is small, deterministic and unit-tested.

3.1 Two-pass tile scoring WordleEngine.evaluate · O(n)

Definition

A multi-pass algorithm walks the data more than once, each pass doing one job, so later passes can rely on what earlier passes decided.

Why it matters here

This is the heart of the puzzle: turning a guess into green (correct), yellow (in the word, wrong place) and grey (not in the word), and it must handle repeated letters correctly.

How we applied it
Pass 1: for each position i
          if guess[i] == answer[i] → Correct (green)
          else count answer[i] in remaining
Pass 2: for each position not yet green
          if remaining[guess[i]] > 0 → SemiCorrect (yellow), remaining −1
          else → Wrong (grey)

Example: answer SISIG, guess SINGS → S I green, N grey, G yellow, the second S yellow (exactly what the booth screenshot shows).

In plain words
First mark every letter that's in the exact right spot. Then, with the letters left over, mark the ones that exist somewhere else, and never more times than they appear in the answer.
Insights

The original Capstone 1 code used a one-pass indexOf(), which matched the first occurrence and showed too many yellows for repeated letters (defect D-01). Two passes plus a count map fix it.

Time is O(n) for an n-letter word: two passes, each with constant-time map lookups. That's instant even for long dish names.

Panel might ask: "Why two passes?" Because greens must be claimed first; otherwise a yellow could use up a letter that a later position needs for a green.

3.2 The XP formula ScoreRules.pointsFor

Definition

A deterministic scoring function: the same inputs always give the same output, with no randomness.

Why it matters here

XP must reward skill fairly and be reproducible: the same round always scores the same on any phone, which the acceptance criteria call "consistent across sessions".

How we applied it
XP = 100                        // solved the dish
   + 20 × (6 − tries used)      // tries left over
   + 50 if solved on the 1st try
   + 60 (<30 s) / 40 (<60 s) / 20 (<120 s) / 0

Range: 100 (last try, slow) to 310 (first try, under 30 s). Losses and replays score 0.

In plain words
You get 100 for solving it, plus bonuses for needing fewer tries, nailing it first time, and being quick.
Insights

Bonuses use buckets (under 30 s, under 60 s…) rather than exact seconds, which the code comments say makes a score "easy to explain to a player, and easy to re-derive if the chain ever needs to audit it".

3.3 Multi-key sorting LeaderboardRules.rank

Definition

Sorting by a primary key and breaking ties with secondary keys, using a stable comparison sort (O(n log n)).

Why it matters here

A leaderboard must give everyone a clear, fair position, even when two players have the same points.

How we applied it
rows.sortedWith(
  compareByDescending { it.points }      // 1. most XP
    .thenByDescending { it.correctCount } // 2. more dishes solved
    .thenBy { it.name.lowercase() })      // 3. A–Z, so ties are stable
In plain words
Rank by score; if tied, whoever solved more dishes goes higher; if still tied, alphabetical.
Insights

The final alphabetical key means the order never "flickers" between refreshes for tied players, which players would otherwise read as a bug.

3.4 Threshold lookup & gated progression ChefRank · UnlockManager

Definition

Mapping a number onto ordered ranges (thresholds), and a state machine that only allows the next step when the current one is finished.

Why it matters here

Ranks and level unlocking are the progression that motivates players (objective 2).

How we applied it
  • ChefRank: Kusinero 0 → Line Cook 5 → Sous Chef 10 → Head Chef 20 dishes → Kusina Master when every visible dish is solved. The steps match the round badges, so a rank and its badge arrive together.
  • UnlockManager.unlockNextLevel() unlocks only level N+1, and only if the level just won is the current highest N.
In plain words
Like a cooking school: you move from apprentice to head chef as you master more dishes, and each class opens only after you pass the one before.
Insights

Ranks count dishes solved, not XP, so a rank reflects how much of the cuisine you've learned, not how fast you type. "Kusina Master" is tied to the total of visible dishes, so it adjusts automatically when admins add levels.

3.6 Hashing & digital signatures SHA-256 · ECDSA

Definition

A cryptographic hash (SHA-256) turns any data into a fixed 64-character fingerprint; changing one character changes the whole fingerprint. A digital signature (ECDSA) proves who created a message and that it wasn't altered.

Why it matters here

They are the algorithms that make the reward ledger trustworthy.

How we applied it
  • Each receipt's id is '0x' . hash('sha256', kk_canonical_json($receipt)): canonical JSON first, so the same receipt always hashes the same.
  • Blocks are signed with openssl_sign(…, OPENSSL_ALGO_SHA256) using an elliptic-curve key on secp256k1 (the curve Ethereum and Polygon use), with prime256v1 as a fallback on servers without it.
  • Custodial wallet addresses are derived from the public key's SHA-256 hash.
In plain words
The hash is a fingerprint of the receipt; the signature is the system's stamp. Change the receipt and the fingerprint won't match; fake the stamp and it won't verify.
Insights

Canonical JSON matters: the same data with keys in a different order would otherwise produce a different hash and look "tampered". The unit suite (api/tests/run.php) checks these primitives. It has grown to 391 checks across all four pipeline phases, all passing on October 13 and again in QA Round 2 on October 19, 2026.

Unit 4

Cybersecurity

Security follows the CIA triad: confidentiality (only the right people see data), integrity (data isn't changed without detection) and availability (the system stays usable). Each module below protects one or more of the three.

4.1 Authentication bcrypt · bearer tokens · Google · Entra

Definition

Proving a user is who they claim to be, and keeping that proof safe afterwards.

Why it matters here

Accounts hold KK, badges and progress. A stolen account is stolen rewards.

How we applied it
  • Passwords are stored only as bcrypt hashes (password_hash(…, PASSWORD_DEFAULT)) and checked with password_verify; the plain password is never stored.
  • After login the server issues a random token (random_bytes), stores only its hash in api_tokens.token_hash, and expires it after 30 days. Logging out deletes it.
  • The phone sends it as Authorization: Bearer … (KtorClient).
  • Players can sign in with Google (verified server-side); admins sign in to the console with Microsoft Entra.
  • Every sign-up and sign-in is also recorded on the ledger. After a successful sign-in, the API sends a 0 KK event, account_signup for a new account or account_login for a returning one, through the same four-phase reward pipeline (login.php, register.php, auth/google.php). It becomes a signed, hash-linked block with its own receipt, and the app's reward history lists it as "Account Login" or "Account Sign Up", marked Secured instead of a KK amount.
In plain words
Your password is turned into an unreadable fingerprint before it's saved. After you log in, you get a temporary pass, and the server only keeps a fingerprint of that too.
Insights

Hashing tokens server-side means even a leaked database can't be used to log in as players. Before the 17 Aug security review, signing out left tokens valid for 30 days (D-25); now logout revokes them.

The blockchain does not decide who can sign in: the password check, the Google check, the token and the lockout all use the database. The ledger only records that a sign-in happened, so account activity has the same tamper-evident trail as rewards (the "activity records" of Objective C). The code says why: the same ledger entry is written whichever way a player signs in, so the audit log reads the same.

4.2 Authorization identity from the token, never the request

Definition

Deciding what an authenticated user is allowed to do. The server must derive "who you are" from a trusted credential, not from what the client says.

Why it matters here

Every reward is tied to a user id. If a client could send any user_id, it could write progress into someone else's account.

How we applied it
  • Endpoints call kk_require_user(), which reads the bearer token and ignores any user_id in the body (fix for D-14, then D-17 for the last four endpoints).
  • Admin console: every page re-checks the signed-in admin and their allowed sections (AdminAuth.IsActiveAdmin), fails closed if unsure, and signs out after 15 minutes idle.
  • Players never touch wallet keys: wallets are custodial, managed on the server.
In plain words
The server doesn't believe "I'm player 46". It checks your pass and decides for itself who you are.
Insights

D-14 was rated Critical: "identity was a claim rather than a credential". It's a classic example of broken access control, the top risk on the OWASP Top 10.

4.3 Brute-force & enumeration defence lockout · rate limits · generic errors

Definition

Limiting repeated guesses and avoiding messages that reveal whether an account exists.

Why it matters here

Login and password-reset codes can be guessed by a script if attempts are unlimited.

How we applied it
  • Every attempt is logged in auth_attempts; the 4th wrong password within 15 minutes, or the 6th wrong reset code, locks that account for 15 minutes (HTTP 429), as tested on a local copy on October 13 and 19 and repeated on the live API ("Too many attempts. Try again in … minutes").
  • Lockout is per account, not per network (D-33): one person's mistakes on campus Wi-Fi don't lock everyone else.
  • Reset codes are bcrypt-hashed and never returned in the API (D-20). Sign-up no longer says which emails exist (D-22), and database errors aren't shown to clients (D-23).
In plain words
Get the password wrong too many times and that account takes a short break. And the app never confirms whether an email is registered.
Insights

Returning the reset code in the API response (D-20) meant anyone could reset anyone's password: a full account takeover. It was found by a security review, not by a user, which is why reviews are part of testing.

4.4 Data protection TLS · AES-256 · secrets · privacy

Definition

Keeping data confidential in transit (encryption over the network) and at rest (encryption on disk), and keeping secrets out of code.

Why it matters here

Tokens, emails and wallet keys pass through phones, Wi-Fi networks and a public GitHub repository.

How we applied it
  • In transit: HTTPS with Let's Encrypt certificates (certbot on the API VM); the app's network_security_config governs its connections.
  • At rest on the phone: the token lives in EncryptedSharedPreferences (AES-256-GCM, key in the Android Keystore), and allowBackup="false" keeps it out of device backups (D-24).
  • At rest on the server: reward payloads use AES-256-GCM; the master key and wallet private keys live in a vault (lib/vault.php), "never in source, in SQL".
  • Key Vault (as of October 19, 2026): the console's secrets and connection string and the API's database password are read from Azure Key Vault. The reward encryption, validator, and reward keys remain on the VM disk, readable only by the server account, with an encrypted copy kept off the machine: the one open finding of the cloud test.
  • Repo hygiene: keystore and passwords are git-ignored; Azure resource paths were masked before publishing test data.
  • Privacy: UAT responses are confidential under the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173).
In plain words
Everything travels in a locked envelope, the phone keeps your pass in a safe, and the server's master keys are never written into the code.
Insights

GCM mode adds authentication on top of encryption: the test suite checks that "tampering ciphertext fails AES-256-GCM authentication", so altered data is rejected rather than silently decrypted into garbage.

4.5 Injection prevention prepared statements

Definition

SQL injection is when user input is treated as part of a database command. Prepared statements send the command and the data separately, so input can never become code.

Why it matters here

Usernames, guesses, report messages and admin form fields all reach the database.

How we applied it

64 PHP files on the live API use PDO prepare() with named parameters, e.g. $pdo->prepare("DELETE FROM api_tokens WHERE token_hash = :h"). The guess validator also accepts letters only (NotLetters).

In plain words
The database gets the question and the answer in separate envelopes, so someone typing a command into the name box just gets a strange name, not a deleted table.
Insights

Injection is a long-standing entry on the OWASP Top 10. Validating on the phone is for user experience; the server's prepared statements are the real defence, because attackers can skip the app entirely.

4.6 Ledger integrity & auditing hash chain · PoA · hourly check

Definition

Integrity means data can't be changed without detection. Proof of Authority (PoA) is a blockchain model where approved signers (here, the server) create blocks, instead of public mining.

Why it matters here

KK and badges should not be editable by anyone, admins included. This is the study's core contribution.

How we applied it
  • Each reward is encrypted, hashed and signed (kk_phase2_assemble_sign_poa), then appended to the hash-linked chain under a lock so two writes can't collide.
  • The admin Audit page lists the whole ledger, and an hourly whole-chain check shows a Chain integrity card.
  • The phone only ever shows balances and status read from the API: no seed phrases, keys or gas fees (a project non-negotiable).
In plain words
Every reward is written in ink in a numbered, fingerprinted book. If anyone erases a line, the hourly check spots it.
Insights

Why PoA instead of a public chain like Ethereum? No gas fees per reward, instant confirmation, and no player wallets to lose, while still keeping the tamper-evidence that the study needs. secp256k1 keys keep the door open to a Polygon-compatible chain later.

Panel might ask: "Can an admin give themselves KK?" They could edit the database, but not the chain; the receipt would be missing or the hashes would break, and the integrity check would flag it.

5.0a What cloud computing is NIST definition · deployment models

Definition

NIST SP 800-145 defines cloud computing as on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources (servers, storage, networks, applications) that can be provisioned and released quickly with minimal management effort.

5 characteristics
  • On-demand self-service: create a server yourself, no ticket to a person.
  • Broad network access: reached over the internet from any device.
  • Resource pooling: many customers share the provider's hardware (multi-tenancy).
  • Rapid elasticity: add or remove capacity quickly.
  • Measured service: you pay for what you use, metered.
Deployment models
  • Public cloud: shared provider infrastructure (Azure, AWS, Google Cloud).
  • Private cloud: infrastructure for one organization only.
  • Community cloud: shared by organizations with common needs.
  • Hybrid cloud: public and private combined.

Kusina Kode runs on the public cloud (Microsoft Azure). Note: the manuscript's "hybrid deployment model" means a mix of service models (IaaS and PaaS), not a hybrid cloud.

In plain words
Renting a kitchen by the hour instead of building one: you get it when you need it, share the building with others, and pay for the hours you cook.

Panel might ask: "Is your system on a hybrid cloud?" No. It is on the public cloud. "Hybrid" in Chapter 3 refers to using two service models, IaaS for the API and PaaS for the console and data.

5.0b How the cloud runs virtualization · regions · VMs vs containers vs serverless

Virtualization

A hypervisor splits one physical server into many isolated virtual machines, each with its own operating system. This is what makes resource pooling possible.

Compute options
  • Virtual machine (IaaS): a full computer you manage, OS included. Ours: the API VM.
  • Container: packages an app with its libraries and shares the host OS kernel; lighter than a VM (Docker, Kubernetes). Not used by Kusina Kode.
  • Platform service (PaaS): you deploy code, the provider runs the server. Ours: App Service for the console.
  • Serverless (FaaS): functions that run on events and bill per execution (Azure Functions). Not used.
Regions and zones

A region is a geographic area with data centers (ours: Southeast Asia). An availability zone is a separate data center within a region; spreading copies across zones survives a data-center failure.

Kusina Kode runs one VM in one region, so the VM is a single point of failure; data survived the September 25 restart, but there is no second copy of the API running.

In plain words
One big building (server) divided into private apartments (VMs). Containers are rooms that share the same plumbing. Serverless is a vending machine: it runs only when someone presses a button.

Panel might ask: "Why not containers or serverless?" The ledger needs a private folder on disk and scheduled jobs, which a VM gives directly; containers or functions would need extra storage and scheduling services. It is a valid future option.

5.0c Running well in the cloud scaling · availability · shared responsibility · cost

Scalability vs elasticity

Scalability is the ability to handle more load by adding resources: vertical (a bigger machine or tier) or horizontal (more machines behind a load balancer). Elasticity is doing it automatically as demand rises and falls (auto-scaling).

Kusina Kode: the stress test showed the Basic database tier is the limit, so the next step is vertical scaling of the database; no auto-scaling is set up.

Availability and SLA

Availability is the share of time a service is up. A Service Level Agreement is the provider's promise, often written in "nines": 99.9% allows about 8.8 hours of downtime a year, 99.99% about 53 minutes. Redundancy (more copies, more zones) raises availability.

Shared responsibility

Security is split between provider and customer. The provider always secures the physical data center and hardware. With IaaS you secure the OS, patches, network rules, and app (we patched the VM's 25 pending updates on September 25). With PaaS the provider also patches the OS and runtime; you secure your code, data, and access.

Cost model

Cloud turns capital expense (buying servers) into operating expense (paying monthly for use). Costs depend on size and tier: a bigger database tier costs more per month, which is the trade-off of scaling.

Monitoring

Metrics (CPU, memory, database load, response time) and logs show health and limits. We used Azure Monitor during the stress test: database at 91%, VM processor at about 12%.

Panel might ask: "How would you handle 1,000 players?" Raise the database tier first (the measured bottleneck), then add a second API VM behind a load balancer and move the ledger's files to shared storage. That is horizontal scaling.

6.1 Data flow diagram (DFD) Gane & Sarson · Levels 0, 1, 2

What it is

A DFD shows how data moves through a system: where it comes from, which process changes it, and where it is stored. It does not show order, timing or decisions; that is what the flowcharts are for.

It is drawn in levels. Level 0 (context diagram) is the whole system as one process. Level 1 opens that box into the main processes. Level 2 opens one Level 1 process further (ours explodes 4.0 Process Rewards into 4.1 to 4.5).

Symbols
SymbolNameWhat it means
External entityA person or outside system that sends or receives data but is not part of the system (E1 Player, E3 Microsoft Entra ID). Also called a source or sink.
ProcessSomething that changes data. Numbered by level: 1.0 at Level 1, 4.1 at Level 2. Named with a verb: "Authenticate & Authorize".
Data storeData at rest: a table group, a file or a folder (D1 Accounts, D5 Reward Ledger). Named with a noun.

  • Every process has at least one input and one output (no "black holes" or "miracles").
  • Data never moves store to store, entity to store, or entity to entity without a process in between.
  • Balancing: the flows into and out of a Level 1 process must match its Level 2 diagram.
  • Labels are nouns (what the data is), never verbs (that is the process's job).
  • Other notation you may see: Yourdon/DeMarco draws processes as circles and stores as two parallel lines. Same meaning.

What it is

A data pipeline is a chain of stages that data passes through, where each stage takes the output of the one before. Two common kinds:

  • Transaction lifecycle (event processing): one record moves through fixed phases and states, like our reward pipeline.
  • Batch ETL: scheduled jobs Extract data from a source, Transform it (sort, count, check), Load the result somewhere fast to read, and then serve it, like our reporting pipeline.
Symbols
SymbolNameWhat it means
StageOne step of the pipeline, in order from left to right.
Data storeWhere a stage reads or writes (D5 Reward ledger, D6 Keys).
Hand-offThe record or data passed to the next stage ("Hands on: tx_hash and the receipt").
Failure pathDashed: what happens if the stage fails.

Our diagrams
  • Phase 1, capture, encrypt, hash: the event is sealed with AES-256-GCM and fingerprinted with SHA-256. State: pending.
  • Phase 2, assemble, sign, validate: a block is built and signed with the validator's ECDSA key, and the PoA rule checks it.
  • Phase 3, append and verify: the block is added to blocks.jsonl, linked to the one before.
  • Phase 4, execute the contract: KKCoin and KKBadge rules update the balance, write the TxHash receipt, and grant a badge or shop item. State: confirmed.
  • Failures: each phase has its own failure path (dashed); a failed record is kept and listed in the audit log. Phase 4 refuses a debit that would go below zero, or a payout when the rewards pool is empty.
  • Every minute: read every receipt, sort and add up, and write receipts_cache.json and ledger_totals.json for the Audit log, KK Coins and Dashboard pages.
  • Every hour: verify the whole chain (position, link, hash and signature of each block) and write verify_status.json.

7.1 Research design and statistics

Concepts the panel tests when you defend your method and results.

What is a descriptive-evaluative research design?

It describes the system as built and evaluates it against set standards (ISO/IEC 25010, SUS, test cases) with numbers, without manipulating variables or comparing to a control group.

Ours is also developmental: we built the system, then evaluated it.

Purposive vs convenience sampling?

Purposive: chosen on purpose for a trait (the expert; culinary and HM students who know the cuisine). Convenience: whoever is available (Cohort B, other programs).

Neither is random, so results describe these respondents and are indicative, not generalizable to all students.

Why a four-point Likert scale?

With no neutral middle, respondents must lean agree or disagree; a midpoint can become a safe default (Kusmaryono et al., 2022).

Interpretation: 3.26–4.00 Strongly Agree · 2.51–3.25 Agree · 1.76–2.50 Disagree · 1.00–1.75 Strongly Disagree.

What is a weighted mean?

The average of ratings where each scale value is multiplied by how many chose it: Σ(value × frequency) ÷ total responses.

Overall ISO/IEC 25010 = 3.70 across 61 respondents.

How is SUS computed?

For the 10 items (1–5): odd items score (answer − 1); even items score (5 − answer). Add the ten, multiply by 2.5 → 0 to 100.

68 is the commonly used average; ours: 70.6 (all 61). It is not a percentage.

Why Mann-Whitney U and not a t-test?

The data are ordinal ratings and the groups are small (20 and 40), so a rank-based, non-parametric test is appropriate (Field, 2018). A t-test assumes interval data and normal distributions.

What does p = .220 mean?

If there were truly no difference, a difference at least this large would appear about 22% of the time. Since p > .05, we found no significant difference.

It does not prove the groups are equal. "Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence."

What is an effect size (r)?

How big a difference is, regardless of sample size: .10 small, .30 medium, .50 large (Cohen, 1988). Ours: .16 (ISO) and .05 (SUS), both small.

Spearman vs Pearson correlation?

Pearson measures a straight-line relationship between interval values; Spearman correlates the ranks, so it suits ordinal data and outliers. Ours: ρ = .59, a strong positive relationship.

ρ² = .35: about a third of the variation is shared. Correlation is not causation.

Why screen out careless responses?

Answering the same option for all ten SUS items gives exactly 50.0 regardless of opinion (insufficient effort responding; Huang et al., 2012). We flagged runs of 7+ identical answers (Johnson, 2005), kept all data, and report the 81 before screening in Appendix U.

Acceptance vs effectiveness?

Acceptance = users rate the system positively and can use it (what we measured). Effectiveness = it improves learning, which needs a pre-test and post-test design (not measured; recommended).

ISO/IEC 25010 has eight characteristics. Why only five?prepare

Suggested answer: we evaluated the five that users can judge from using the app (functional suitability, performance efficiency, usability, reliability, security). Compatibility was tested by the team on browsers and devices, and maintainability and portability concern the code rather than the user.

Not written in the manuscript. Chapter 3 names the five but gives no reason; agree on this answer as a group or add a sentence to Chapter 3.

7.2 Software engineering and testing

Method and testing vocabulary.

Scrum vs Waterfall?

Waterfall runs phases once in sequence (requirements → design → build → test). Scrum repeats short sprints that each deliver a working, tested increment, so feedback changes the next sprint.

Scrum roles and events?

Roles: Product Owner (Chef Gicana), Scrum Master (Jhan Maurice), Development Team. Events: sprint planning, the sprint, sprint review, sprint retrospective. Artifacts: product backlog, sprint backlog, increment.

Unit vs integration vs system vs acceptance testing?

Unit: one function in isolation. Integration: parts working together (app ↔ API). System: the whole deployed system (stress, security, cloud, compatibility). Acceptance: the user or expert confirms it meets needs (our 23 expert test cases).

Load vs stress testing?

Load testing checks behaviour at expected load; stress testing pushes beyond it to find the breaking point. Ours ramped 5 → 40 players; no request failed and the database was the limit.

SAST vs DAST?

Static (SAST) reads the source code without running it (Semgrep). Dynamic (DAST) attacks the running application from outside (OWASP ZAP).

What is regression testing, and CI?

Regression testing re-runs tests after a change to catch new breakage. Continuous integration runs the tests automatically on every change; ours run on GitHub Actions for every pull request.

What is MVVM?

Model-View-ViewModel: the View (screen) shows state, the ViewModel holds state and handles actions, the Model holds data and rules. Screens never call the network directly.

What is a REST API?

An interface over HTTP where URLs name resources and methods state the action (GET read, POST create, PUT/PATCH update, DELETE remove), usually exchanging JSON. Ours: the PHP REST API at api.kusinakode.com.

HTTP 200, 401, 403, 429?

200 OK. 401 Unauthorized: not signed in or bad token. 403 Forbidden: signed in but not allowed. 429 Too Many Requests: our lockout after too many failed attempts.

7.3 Security and blockchain

The most-tested area, because blockchain is the main contribution.

Hashing vs encryption?

Encryption is reversible with a key (AES-256 hides the reward data). Hashing is one-way: SHA-256 gives a fingerprint that cannot be turned back into the data, used to detect changes.

Symmetric vs asymmetric cryptography?

Symmetric uses one shared key to encrypt and decrypt (AES). Asymmetric uses a key pair: private to sign or decrypt, public to verify or encrypt (ECDSA signatures).

What does a digital signature prove?

Authenticity (it came from the holder of the private key, our validator) and integrity (it was not changed). It also gives non-repudiation.

Why are passwords hashed with bcrypt?

bcrypt is deliberately slow and salted, so stolen hashes are expensive to guess (about 66 ms per hash in our test). A fast hash like SHA-256 would let attackers try billions per second.

Does the app use the blockchain to sign in?

Not to check who you are. Sign-in is a bcrypt-checked password or a verified Google token, then a session token, all in the database. After it succeeds, the API records a 0 KK account_login (or account_signup) event on the ledger through the four-phase pipeline, with its own receipt.

Say: "The blockchain doesn't authenticate the user; it records the sign-in, so account activity is as tamper-evident as rewards." In the app it appears in the reward history as "Account Login", marked Secured.

Authentication vs authorization?

Authentication: who are you (password, Google, Entra). Authorization: what you may do (your token's identity, admin section permissions).

What is the CIA triad?

Confidentiality (only the right people see data), Integrity (data is not changed undetected), Availability (the system stays usable). Our cybersecurity architecture is organized by it.

Proof-of-Work vs Proof-of-Stake vs Proof-of-Authority?

PoW: miners spend computing power (Bitcoin). PoS: validators lock up coins (Ethereum today). PoA: approved, known validators sign blocks; fast and free, but trust rests on the validators. Ours: PoA with one validator.

Tamper-evident vs tamper-proof vs decentralized?

Tamper-evident: changes are detected (ours: hash links and signatures, verified hourly). Tamper-proof: changes are impossible. Decentralized: no single party controls the ledger. Ours is tamper-evident, not decentralized.

What is a smart contract, and do you have one?

Code that automatically executes rules on a blockchain. Our KKCoin and KKBadge "contract rules" run in the API in Phase 4; they are not deployed on a public chain.

What is a custodial wallet?

The platform holds the keys for the user. Players need no seed phrase, wallet app, or gas fees; the trade-off is that players trust the platform.

What is SQL injection, and how are you protected?

Input crafted to change a database command (' OR '1'='1). We use prepared statements with bound parameters, so input is never treated as code; tests refused the payloads and the users table stayed intact.

What is zero trust?

"Never trust, always verify": every request is checked, regardless of where it comes from. Every protected endpoint verifies the bearer token (Chandramouli & Butcher, 2025).

7.4 Data, algorithms, and architecture

Short definitions you should say in one breath.

1NF, 2NF, 3NF?

1NF: one value per cell, no repeating groups. 2NF: 1NF and every non-key column depends on the whole key. 3NF: 2NF and no column depends on another non-key column. We moved comma-separated lists into child tables (October 12–13).

Primary key vs foreign key?

A primary key uniquely identifies a row; a foreign key points to another table's primary key to link them. A composite key uses several columns (reward_claims: user, kind, claim key).

What is O(n)? O(1)? O(n log n)?

How running time grows with input size n. Tile scoring is O(n) (two passes over the word); a hash map lookup is O(1) on average; the leaderboard sort is O(n log n).

Why two passes in tile scoring?

Greens must be claimed first; otherwise a yellow could use up a letter that a later position needs for a green. This fixed the repeated-letter defect (D-01).

What is a hash-linked chain?

A list where each block stores the hash of the previous block; changing any block breaks every later link. It is the core data structure of a blockchain.

Clean Architecture in one line?

Inner layers (business rules) never depend on outer layers (database, UI), so rules can be tested alone and outer parts swapped. Used in both the app and the console.

Problem, rationale, and contribution

What problem does Kusina Kode solve?

Filipino culinary heritage has no structured, engaging digital learning tool: content is scattered, popular apps are international, and gamified apps keep rewards as editable records. Kusina Kode combines expert-validated Filipino dish content, a word puzzle, gamification, and verifiable rewards in one platform.

Evidence: pre-development survey of 64 (mobile apps least used, 7.8%; 89.1% valued expert-validated recipe information), the expert interview (no Filipino-focused app; Mindanao underrepresented), and the literature on editable reward records (Motepalli & Jacobsen, 2021).

What is the main contribution of the study?

The blockchain-based incentive system: every reward goes through encryption, hashing, signing, and contract rules on a private Proof-of-Authority ledger and produces a TxHash receipt, so rewards in an educational game can be verified.

Chapter 5 names objective C as the main contribution. It is integrated with gamified heritage learning, which the reviewed systems (Table 1) do not combine.

Why Filipino culinary content and why gamification?

Culinary heritage carries regional identity and is at risk of fading as recipes receive less attention; gamification is well documented to raise motivation and engagement in education.

Gelera et al. (2023) on fading regional dishes; Oliveira et al. (2023), Jaramillo-Mediavilla et al. (2024), and Lampropoulos & Sidiropoulos (2024) on gamified learning. The puzzle and heritage content give intrinsic motivation; points, badges, and KK give extrinsic motivation (John et al., 2023).

Were all three objectives met?

It is outside our scope, stated in the delimitations. Our evaluation measures acceptance, software quality, and usability. Measuring learning needs a pre-test and post-test over several weeks, which is our final recommendation.

What is your research design?

Quantitative, descriptive-evaluative, with a developmental approach: we built the system in twelve Scrum sprints and evaluated it against ISO/IEC 25010 and the SUS.

To make respondents take a stance; a neutral midpoint can become a safe default (Kusmaryono et al., 2022).

Blockchain

Why use blockchain at all? A database could store rewards.

A normal database row can be changed without trace. Our ledger signs every reward and links each block to the previous one by its hash, so any change is detected when the chain is verified. That gives players and admins verifiable proof of what was earned.

Respondents rated S3 (verifying rewards increases trust) 3.77 and FS8 (coins and badges match what was earned) 3.85, the highest item.

Why a private, permissioned ledger instead of Ethereum or Polygon?

Players should not need a crypto wallet, seed phrase, or gas fees, and KK Coins are not meant to be traded. A private Proof-of-Authority ledger gives tamper-evidence without those costs.

Chapter 2 compares public platforms (Polygon, Ethereum, Solana, Hyperledger Fabric). Custodial wallets keep the experience simple; U6 (rewards usable without knowing blockchain) 3.49 shows players used rewards without needing to understand the ledger.

If your team holds the only validator key, can you not rewrite the chain?

Yes, and we state it: the ledger is tamper-evident, not decentralized. Any silent edit is detected when the chain is verified from its genesis block, but nothing stops the operators from rebuilding it. We recommend independent validators or anchoring block hashes on a public network.

Delimitations (Chapter 1) and Chapter 5. The chain is re-verified every hour; the cloud test verified 4,315 blocks in 6.5 seconds with no broken link or bad signature.

What is Proof-of-Authority?

A consensus method where only approved validators may add blocks; a block is valid only if it carries an approved validator's signature.

Our harness tests that a block signed by a rogue validator and a forged signature are both rejected (391 checks).

Explain encryption, hashing, and digital signatures in your pipeline.

AES-256-GCM encrypts the reward data so it stays private; SHA-256 creates a fingerprint that changes if any bit changes; ECDSA signs the record with the validator's private key, proving it came from our system.

Measured cost: AES-256-GCM 0.021 ms, SHA-256 0.002 ms, ECDSA sign 0.40 ms and verify 0.37 ms (MacBook Air M2), so cryptography is not the bottleneck.

What are the KKCoin and KKBadge contracts? Are they smart contracts?

They are the contract rules our API runs in phase 4 to decide how many KK Coins or which badge a reward produces; they are not deployed on a public blockchain.

Phase 4 applies the rules and issues the TxHash receipt; KK minting and spending are covered by the test harness.

What is a TxHash receipt and how does a player use it?

The unique hash of a recorded reward. Players open it in the wallet as a ledger ticket that shows the reference, date, amount, and "Verified on the ledger"; admins see it in the Audit log.

UAT case D3 (open a transaction's ledger ticket) passed. The Audit log also shows chain totals (E5).

Where are the keys stored? Is that secure?

The console's secrets are in Azure Key Vault. The reward encryption, validator, and reward keys are files on the VM, readable only by the server account, with an encrypted copy held off the machine since October 17. Moving them into Key Vault is our open cloud finding and a recommendation.

Cloud test: 42 of 43 checks passed; this is the one open finding.

Cohort comparison and statistics

Why Mann-Whitney U and not a t-test?

The ratings are ordinal and the groups are small (20 and 40), so a rank-based test is appropriate (Field, 2018).

Ties corrected; effect size r read against Cohen's benchmarks.

What did the cohort comparison show?

No significant difference: ISO/IEC 25010 U = 321.5, p = .220, r = .16; SUS U = 375.5, p = .706, r = .05. Culinary and non-culinary students responded alike, so the 61 can be read as one sample.

With 20 and 40 respondents the test could miss a small difference, so this shows similar acceptance, not proof of equivalence.

Does p = .220 mean the cohorts are the same?

No. It means the data do not show a significant difference at the .05 level. Absence of evidence of a difference is not evidence of equivalence.

An equivalence test would need larger samples and a predefined margin.

What does the Spearman correlation tell us?

ρ = .59, p < .001: respondents who rated quality higher also found the platform easier to use, so the two instruments agree. About a third of the variation is shared (ρ² = .35).

Students only: ρ = .58, so it does not depend on the expert. It is an association, not a cause, and both questionnaires were answered in one session.
